What to expect from a home birth in Portugal as a foreigner

Written by Mina, doula and birth photographer

Published 17 September 2026

Last reviewed 25 September 2026

If you are pregnant and living in Portugal, you may eventually find yourself asking a question:

Can I have my baby at home in Portugal? And what does that actually look like?

The short answer is: homebirth is a legal option in Portugal, but the system around it is not as formally structured as it is in some other European countries.

For a foreigner, this can make the process feel confusing but on the other hand wonderfully personal. You may be used to a clearly defined homebirth pathway in your home country, with registered midwives, established referral systems and straightforward information. In Portugal, you may have to do considerably more of the research and planning yourself.

So, if you are considering a home birth here, this is what you should know.

Homebirth exists in Portugal, but it is not a standardized pathway

Homebirth is a valid option in Portugal, but currently there is no specific legislation regulating home birth as a separate maternity-care pathway.

This means you shouldn't expect a state-organized homebirth service comparable to systems where home birth is integrated into the public maternity system.

Instead, families generally need to find an appropriately qualified professional or team who provides this kind of care. Homebirth in Portugal is not covered by the health insurance, so midwife services have to be financed from your own pocket.

Who attends a homebirth?

In Portugal, the professional you will encounter is an Enfermeiro Especialista em Enfermagem de Saúde Materna e Obstétrica (EEESMO) - a nurse specialist in maternal and obstetric health.

This is the Portuguese professional qualification associated with midwifery practice. The Ordem dos Enfermeiros recognizes Maternal and Obstetric Health Nursing as a specialist nursing field, and its guidance states that EEESMO professionals are qualified to conduct normal birth.

If you are coming from the UK, Australia, Ireland, the Netherlands or another country where the word midwife is familiar, the terminology can be confusing.

You may need to build your own care team

One of the biggest differences a foreigner may notice is the degree of responsibility placed on the family to organize their care.

Unlike in some other European countries where homebirth is part of public health services, in Portugal you will need to find your own homebirth professional, establish a relationship with them during pregnancy and discuss how they work.

Organizing it all on your own can feel overwhelming at first, but it grants you the freedom to choose a midwife who truly aligns with your vision and prioritizes what matters most to you.

The continuity of care has many benefits since the midwife you chose will be familiar with your pregnancy, wishes and birth environment.

You have time to build trust.

You can talk about your previous births, your fears, your preferences, your cultural background and what makes you feel safe.

And if Portuguese isn't your first language, language should absolutely be part of your conversation when choosing your birth team.

Being able to communicate clearly during labour is not a luxury.

Your pregnancy care and your birth can be organized separately

Another thing that can be confusing for foreigners is that prenatal care, birth and postpartum care don't necessarily have to come from one single provider.

You might have your pregnancy followed through:

  • the Portuguese public health system (SNS)
  • a private obstetrician
  • a private clinic
  • an independent midwife/EEESMO
  • or a combination of these
  • not followed at all

Your homebirth midwife will provide continuity of care through your pregnancy, usually offering at least 4 visits before birth, so she can listen to the baby, measure your blood pressure and offer some tests to rule out proteins in the urine etc.

Continuity of care leads to better outcomes since the midwife gets to know what is normal for you. She becomes familiar with your baby's baseline heart rate so she can accurately monitor it during birth itself.

What about safety?

Homebirth is assumed to be riskier than hospital birth, but finally there is a large body of evidence that states otherwise.

The major systematic review and meta-analysis published by The Lancet's EClinicalMedicine journal, shows that there is no difference in risk for mother and baby when the birth is intended to be at home or in hospital.

The review analysed data from roughly 500,000 planned home births among low-risk women and compared it to a similar number of planned low risk hospital births and found that there is no difference in outcomes for mothers and babies.

“The risk of perinatal or neonatal mortality was not different when birth was intended at home or in hospital.” (Hutton et al 2019).

The meta-analysis by Scarf et al (2018) even concluded that there are better chances for birth to unfold naturally, with less interventions, for women who choose homebirth.

Safety isn’t only about the access to facilities, but rather emotional which is the very foundation of birth physiology. That’s why many women chose homebirth, especially if they had previous traumatic experiences in hospital.

What does homebirth look like?

One of the biggest advantages of home birth is that you never have to leave your own space. Everything is already familiar, and you can shape the atmosphere exactly the way you like it - the lighting, the sounds, the smells, people present. You already know where everything is, so if you need something, you simply get up and get it, rather than asking permission or waiting for someone else to bring it to you.

At home, you can fully surrender to birth itself. Everyone present is there to adapt to your needs and wishes, not the other way around.

Your birth space also doesn't need to be sterile, because you're the only one giving birth there. You're already used to the microorganisms in your own home. If it's clean enough for sex and menstruate, it's clean enough for birth too.

You might labour in your bedroom, living room, bathroom or wherever you feel comfortable. Women tend to prepare beautiful environments, candles, a birth pool, string lights, and end up giving birth in the narrow space between the bed and the closet. We are mammals, after all, and like most mammals, we tend to seek out small, secluded, enclosed spaces when it's time to give birth

Useful things:

  • Pilates ball
  • Absorbing pads 60x60
  • Waterproof mattress protector
  • Clean towels
  • Yoga mat
  • pillows
  • dim lighting (camping lamps, string lights)
  • Music
  • Birth pool

How to register a baby?

If your baby is born at home in Portugal, there is also the practical matter of registering the birth.

The Portuguese government states that every child born in Portugal must be registered, including children whose parents are foreigners.

To register a homebirth baby in Portugal, you must book an appointment or submit a declaration at a local Civil Registry Office (Conservatória do Registo Civil) within 20 days of the birth.

Both parents should ideally attend. If unmarried, the father must be present to acknowledge paternity (perfilhação)

Documentation:

  • Valid identification (passports or Citizen Cards) for both parents
  • Proof of address in Portugal
  • Birth documentation provided by your registered midwife

If you had an unassisted birth you will need additional documentation:

  • Prenatal Care Records (Boletim de Saúde da Grávida) if you have one
  • You must bring two adult Portuguese speaking witnesses with valid photo IDs to the Conservatória to testify under oath that they observed the pregnancy and/or the home birth.
